## 1. OSCAL: relevant, but materially different

The sentence “Which is what the specification said did not exist” is wrong.

OSCAL provides:

- machine-readable assessment reports;
- observations, evidence links, findings, logs, and assessor statements;
- identifiers and some hash-bearing resources;
- extensibility sufficient to carry NCP-like data.

But OSCAL does not, by itself, provide the conjunction your original claim described:

- Its “attestations” are “textual statements, typically written by the assessor,” not a restricted machine-enforced claim grammar. [NIST OSCAL reference](https://pages.nist.gov/OSCAL-Reference/models/v1.2.2/assessment-results/xml-definitions/)
- Its properties are deliberately extensible and may carry arbitrary controlled values.
- It does not require a negative-control observation for every reported passing check.
- It does not enforce that positive and fault-injected runs used the identical check artifact.
- It does not enforce capability relevance.
- It does not make broader assurance claims non-conforming.
- Assessment Results are not a bounded standalone artifact in the NCP sense: NIST says they must be associated with an Assessment Plan, which in turn is connected to a system and control framework. [NIST Assessment Results overview](https://pages.nist.gov/OSCAL/learn/concepts/layer/assessment/assessment-results/)

“Machine-readable” has also silently become “machine-checkable.” OSCAL schemas can check structural validity; that is not the same as checking NCP’s semantic admissibility rule.

Therefore:

> OSCAL is close prior art for the container and traceability machinery, but not demonstrated prior art for the NCP artifact as a whole.

Your defensible correction was: “We had not searched; OSCAL covers several ingredients; novelty is unresolved.” It was not: “the artifact claim was wrong.”

## 2. The residual distinction is real—but currently framed as a category error

There is a real distinction between:

1. a format capable of recording assessment evidence; and
2. a conformance regime that makes a result inadmissible unless specified control evidence exists.

That distinction is worth something. Evidence admissibility is stronger than evidence serialization.

But “OSCAL records; NCP constrains” is too clean. OSCAL’s control catalog, profile, assessment plan, and results models can jointly represent requirements and their assessment. A tailored control plus external validation rules could probably implement much of NCP. An OSCAL Profile alone may not enforce the cross-document and cross-run invariants, so “probably expressible as an OSCAL profile” is also premature.

Your question should not be whether a “standard format” expresses the precondition. Formats generally carry data; controls and conformance rules impose policy. Ask instead:

> Does an existing standard or conformance regime make a check’s result inadmissible unless the same check version has demonstrated sensitivity to a declared, relevant challenge condition, with recorded provenance?

That is coherent.

But it is probably not novel as a principle. Laboratory testing already uses exactly this validity structure. NIST defines a positive control as a known sample used to determine whether the test performed properly. [NIST control terminology](https://www.nist.gov/glossary-term/36276) FDA guidance says that when a positive control fails to produce the expected response, specimen results are invalid; some systems invalidate the batch automatically. [FDA IHC guidance](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/guidance-documents-medical-devices-and-radiation-emitting-products/guidance-submission-immunohistochemistry-applications-food-and-drug-administration-final-guidance), [FDA assay example](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/reviews/K111778.pdf)

That is closer structurally than OSCAL:

```text
known challenge not detected
        ↓
measurement/check has not demonstrated sensitivity
        ↓
ordinary result is inadmissible
```

This also reveals a likely naming defect. A deliberately introduced fault that the detector must notice is conventionally closer to a positive control, challenge test, fault-injection test, or proof test. A negative control ordinarily establishes that the procedure does not produce an unintended positive response. Calling the injected fault a “negative control” may itself have caused [REDACTED]’s confusion.

NCP may still contribute a useful software-specific composition:

- per-check rather than per-suite validity;
- exact artifact/version binding;
- capability-relevant perturbation;
- durable third-party evidence;
- explicit limits on downstream claims.

That composition may be useful without being historically novel.

## What it found

**1. Oracle quality is an established research area with its own metrics.** *Oracle deficiency*,
*checked coverage*, and mutation analysis are named techniques for assessing whether an oracle can
detect faults. There is a 2022 survey of oracle-based test adequacy metrics and 2025 work on state
field coverage as an oracle-quality metric. [REDACTED]'s one-line answer pointed at a real literature.

**2. OSCAL is the serious problem for our claim.** NIST's Open Security Controls Assessment
Language has an **Assessment Results model** that carries, in machine-readable XML/JSON/YAML:
assessment scope and timing, assessment subjects, assessment assets (the tools used), an
assessment log of actions performed, **observations containing human or machine-generated evidence
of compliance *or non-compliance***, findings, risks, and **attestations**. It is adopted by
FedRAMP and explicitly designed so that **third-party assessment organisations** can automate
planning, execution and reporting.

That is a bounded, machine-checkable, publishable attestation of executed assessment activity that
a third party can check. **Which is what the specification said did not exist.**

**3. ISO 26262 mandates fault-injection testing** to verify safety mechanisms, with documented
evidence, in a regulated domain. Required fault injection with an evidence trail is closer prior
art for the *attestation* half than mutation testing ever was for the *practice* half.

**N1. Every check has a declared negative control.** For each check in the attestation, a
`negative_control` naming the condition under which the check must fail, and how that condition is
produced.

**N2. The negative control was executed.** Not described, not asserted — run, with a recorded
timestamp.

**N3. The check failed under it.** The recorded outcome under the negative control MUST be a
failure. **A negative control the check survives is a defect in the check**, and the attestation
MUST NOT claim conformance.

**N4. Both runs are bound to the same check identity.** The positive and negative runs MUST name
the same check by a stable id and the same executed artifact (path plus content hash). A negative
control run against a different build, host, or code path proves nothing about the check that
shipped.

**N5. The negative control MUST perturb what the check certifies, not merely the transport.**
Cutting the network makes every check fail and demonstrates nothing about any of them. The
perturbation MUST target the declared capability.

**N6. Skipped, suppressed and unsupported checks are disclosed**, with reasons, and are never
counted as passing. (Carried from AS-02.)

**N7. The claim is bounded.** A conforming attestation may state only:

> Check set *C* was exercised against configuration *X* at time *T*. Each check was observed to
> fail under its declared negative control. This is process evidence about the checks. **It is not
> a claim that the system is safe, aligned, or correct.**

*"We follow OAGF"* and *"NCP certified"* are **non-conforming claims.**

## 5. What conformance does not establish

* **That the negative controls are the right ones.** A check can fail under a trivial perturbation
  and remain blind to the failure that will actually occur. NCP raises the floor from *never
  observed to fail* to *observed to fail at least once*. That is a floor, not an assurance.
* **That the check set covers the capability.** N5 constrains each control's relevance; nothing
  here constrains coverage.
* **Anything about the system.** Every claim is about the checks.
* **That the attestation is honest.** A self-issued attestation is self-issued. NCP makes the
  specific lie *"this check works"* harder to tell by accident; it does nothing about lying on
  purpose.

## "Test oracle" — a prior-art term this project had not considered

The suggestion is not a match for the artifact, but it is close enough to be worth taking
seriously, and it is the first externally-supplied term in this project's history.

A **test oracle** is the mechanism that decides whether an observed output is correct. NCP is not
about the oracle's decision; it is about whether the oracle **can decide wrongly in the failing
direction** — whether it has ever been observed to fail. In the testing literature that concern
has names: *oracle quality*, *oracle deficiency*, *mutation score* as a proxy for it.

So the reply reads as though the question landed as *"what do you call the thing that checks the
output?"* rather than *"what do you call a publishable attestation that the checker was observed
failing?"* Whether that is a defect in the question or a real answer we misread is **not yet
established** — and the honest position is that we do not know which.

has ever been recorded.** The claim has always rested on not having found something, which is the
exact shape of the two false absence claims this record has already published.

   117	## 4. Normative requirements
   118	
   119	An attestation conforms to NCP v0.1 if and only if all of the following hold.
   120	
   121	**N1. Every check has a declared negative control.** For each check in the attestation, a
   122	`negative_control` naming the condition under which the check must fail, and how that condition is
   123	produced.
   124	
   125	**N2. The negative control was executed.** Not described, not asserted — run, with a recorded
   126	timestamp.
   127	
   128	**N3. The check failed under it.** The recorded outcome under the negative control MUST be a
   129	failure. **A negative control the check survives is a defect in the check**, and the attestation
   130	MUST NOT claim conformance.
   131	
   132	**N4. Both runs are bound to the same check identity.** The positive and negative runs MUST name
   133	the same check by a stable id and the same executed artifact (path plus content hash). A negative
   134	control run against a different build, host, or code path proves nothing about the check that
   135	shipped.
   136	
   137	**N5. The negative control MUST perturb what the check certifies, not merely the transport.**
   138	Cutting the network makes every check fail and demonstrates nothing about any of them. The
   139	perturbation MUST target the declared capability.
   140	
   141	**N6. Skipped, suppressed and unsupported checks are disclosed**, with reasons, and are never
   142	counted as passing. (Carried from AS-02.)
   143	
   144	**N7. The claim is bounded.** A conforming attestation may state only:
   145	
   146	> Check set *C* was exercised against configuration *X* at time *T*. Each check was observed to
   147	> fail under its declared negative control. This is process evidence about the checks. **It is not
   148	> a claim that the system is safe, aligned, or correct.**
   149	
   150	*"We follow OAGF"* and *"NCP certified"* are **non-conforming claims.**
   151	
   152	## 5. What conformance does not establish
   153

   155	  and remain blind to the failure that will actually occur. NCP raises the floor from *never
   156	  observed to fail* to *observed to fail at least once*. That is a floor, not an assurance.
   157	* **That the check set covers the capability.** N5 constrains each control's relevance; nothing
   158	  here constrains coverage.

   160	* **That the attestation is honest.** A self-issued attestation is self-issued. NCP makes the
   161	  specific lie *"this check works"* harder to tell by accident; it does nothing about lying on
   162	  purpose.

## 1. OSCAL: relevant, but materially different

The sentence “Which is what the specification said did not exist” is wrong.

OSCAL provides:

- machine-readable assessment reports;
- observations, evidence links, findings, logs, and assessor statements;
- identifiers and some hash-bearing resources;
- extensibility sufficient to carry NCP-like data.

But OSCAL does not, by itself, provide the conjunction your original claim described:

- Its “attestations” are “textual statements, typically written by the assessor,” not a restricted machine-enforced claim grammar. [NIST OSCAL reference](https://pages.nist.gov/OSCAL-Reference/models/v1.2.2/assessment-results/xml-definitions/)
- Its properties are deliberately extensible and may carry arbitrary controlled values.
- It does not require a negative-control observation for every reported passing check.
- It does not enforce that positive and fault-injected runs used the identical check artifact.
- It does not enforce capability relevance.
- It does not make broader assurance claims non-conforming.
- Assessment Results are not a bounded standalone artifact in the NCP sense: NIST says they must be associated with an Assessment Plan, which in turn is connected to a system and control framework. [NIST Assessment Results overview](https://pages.nist.gov/OSCAL/learn/concepts/layer/assessment/assessment-results/)

“Machine-readable” has also silently become “machine-checkable.” OSCAL schemas can check structural validity; that is not the same as checking NCP’s semantic admissibility rule.

Therefore:

> OSCAL is close prior art for the container and traceability machinery, but not demonstrated prior art for the NCP artifact as a whole.

Your defensible correction was: “We had not searched; OSCAL covers several ingredients; novelty is unresolved.” It was not: “the artifact claim was wrong.”

The most serious unsupported passages are [the asserted equivalence](/home/reed/git/open-asi-governance-forum/record/findings/2026-08-11-prior-art-search-ncp-artifact.md:44) and [“three times wrong”](/home/reed/git/open-asi-governance-forum/record/findings/2026-08-11-prior-art-search-ncp-artifact.md:78). ## 2. The residual distinction is real—but currently framed as a category error

There is a real distinction between:

1. a format capable of recording assessment evidence; and
2. a conformance regime that makes a result inadmissible unless specified control evidence exists.

That distinction is worth something. Evidence admissibility is stronger than evidence serialization.

But “OSCAL records; NCP constrains” is too clean. OSCAL’s control catalog, profile, assessment plan, and results models can jointly represent requirements and their assessment. A tailored control plus external validation rules could probably implement much of NCP. An OSCAL Profile alone may not enforce the cross-document and cross-run invariants, so “probably expressible as an OSCAL profile” is also premature.

Your question should not be whether a “standard format” expresses the precondition. Formats generally carry data; controls and conformance rules impose policy. Ask instead:

> Does an existing standard or conformance regime make a check’s result inadmissible unless the same check version has demonstrated sensitivity to a declared, relevant challenge condition, with recorded provenance?

That is coherent.

But it is probably not novel as a principle. Laboratory testing already uses exactly this validity structure. NIST defines a positive control as a known sample used to determine whether the test performed properly. [NIST control terminology](https://www.nist.gov/glossary-term/36276) FDA guidance says that when a positive control fails to produce the expected response, specimen results are invalid; some systems invalidate the batch automatically. [FDA IHC guidance](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/guidance-documents-medical-devices-and-radiation-emitting-products/guidance-submission-immunohistochemistry-applications-food-and-drug-administration-final-guidance), [FDA assay example](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/reviews/K111778.pdf)

That is closer structurally than OSCAL:

```text
known challenge not detected
        ↓
measurement/check has not demonstrated sensitivity
        ↓
ordinary result is inadmissible
```

This also reveals a likely naming defect. A deliberately introduced fault that the detector must notice is conventionally closer to a positive control, challenge test, fault-injection test, or proof test. A negative control ordinarily establishes that the procedure does not produce an unintended positive response. Calling the injected fault a “negative control” may itself have caused [REDACTED]’s confusion.

NCP may still contribute a useful software-specific composition:

- per-check rather than per-suite validity;
- exact artifact/version binding;
- capability-relevant perturbation;
- durable third-party evidence;
- explicit limits on downstream claims.

That composition may be useful without being historically novel.
